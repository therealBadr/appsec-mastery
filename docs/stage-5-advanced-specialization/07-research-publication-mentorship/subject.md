# Security Research, Publication, and Mentorship: The Subject

*Subject 7 of 7 — Advanced Specialization · Version 1.0 — September 2026*

> The roadmap's closing subject, matching its own final-stage guidance exactly: present at a conference or community event, write genuinely research-level content, and mentor others — because teaching what you've mastered is, by this roadmap's own repeated rule, the only reliable way to find out what you don't actually understand yet.

## Foreword

> *Never move on from a topic you can't teach. That rule, stated in the very first stage of this roadmap, was always pointing here.*

## Why This Subject

Every subject before this one built a skill. This one spends it outward — turning two years (or however long this roadmap actually took, since it was never on a clock) of accumulated depth into a conference talk, a piece of research-level writing, and mentorship for someone earlier on the same path you just walked, which is also, not incidentally, the single best remaining way to discover the gaps your own understanding still has.

## What You Must Understand

### Choosing a talk or research topic

The strongest material is almost always something you built and understand at a depth few others in the room will match — a capstone tool's design decisions, a CVE reproduction's independent analysis, or an original synthesis pattern noticed while connecting two vulnerability classes across this roadmap.

- A talk about a tool you built and shipped (Tool Building and Publishing) has a natural, concrete structure: problem, existing approaches' gaps, your design, what you'd change now
- Original synthesis (the same standard Professional Security Writing held blog posts to) makes for stronger conference material than a walkthrough of a well-known technique

### Submitting to and presenting at a conference or meetup

The practical mechanics: a strong CFP (Call for Papers) abstract states the concrete takeaway a busy reviewer can evaluate in one read, local/community meetups are a legitimate, lower-barrier first venue exactly as valid as a large conference, and rehearsal against a real audience beats rehearsing alone every time.

- A CFP abstract that promises a specific, concrete takeaway ("how to build X" or "why Y class of bug keeps recurring in Z") gets accepted more often than one promising a general topic area
- Local meetups and community events are the recommended first venue — lower stakes, faster feedback loop, and exactly what the roadmap's own Stage 5 guidance names first

### Writing research-level content

A step beyond Professional Security Writing's blog-post standard: a new attack technique, a tool that solves a previously-unsolved problem, or a real-world breach analysis written with the rigor and citation discipline of a paper, not just a well-structured blog post.

- A real-world breach analysis done well requires the same evidence-based, no-unsupported-assertion discipline every capstone in this roadmap has been graded on, applied to public incident reporting instead of your own lab
- Citing and engaging with prior work in the area (a related CVE, a related technique from a named researcher) situates original work honestly instead of presenting a known idea as more novel than it is

### Mentorship

Teaching someone earlier on this exact path forces you to make implicit knowledge explicit — the same "if you can't teach it, you don't know it yet" standard from this roadmap's very first Personal Operating System rule, now pointed at a real student instead of yourself.

- A mentee's question you can't answer cleanly is a direct, immediate signal of a gap in your own understanding — treat it as the highest-value kind of feedback, not an embarrassment
- Structuring a mentee's early path (a lighter version of this same roadmap's progression) is itself a test of whether you understand the field's actual dependency structure, not just its individual facts

## Practical Outputs

!!! success "Practical outputs"

    - A conference or meetup talk actually submitted (and, ideally, delivered) on a topic drawn from your own roadmap work — abstract, slides, and a recorded or live delivery.
    - One piece of research-level writing (a new technique, an unsolved-problem-solving tool writeup, or a rigorous breach analysis) meeting a higher citation/rigor bar than your regular blog output from Professional Security Writing.
    - A documented mentorship relationship: at least one person you've actively helped navigate an early stage of this same field, with a written note on what their questions revealed about gaps in your own understanding.
    - A final personal retrospective on the entire roadmap: which stage most changed how you think, which exit gate was hardest to actually clear honestly, and what "AppSec mastery" means to you now, compared to what you assumed it meant back at Stage 0.

## Common Delusions

!!! warning "Common delusions at this stage"

    - "I'm not senior enough to present or mentor yet." The roadmap's own guidance places this at the end specifically because depth, not seniority or tenure, is the actual prerequisite — and you've just spent five stages building exactly that.
    - "Mentoring takes time away from my own growth." It's one of the few activities left at this stage that reliably surfaces gaps in your own understanding — treat it as advanced training, not a pure donation of time.
    - "Research-level writing means it has to be a completely novel discovery." A rigorous, well-cited, independently-verified analysis of an existing technique or incident clears this bar — novelty is one route to research-level work, not the only one.

## Exit Gate

!!! abstract "Exit gate: all of it, without notes"

    - Present your conference/meetup talk live, handling audience questions you didn't prepare for.
    - Defend your research-level writing's rigor: sourcing, verification, and honest positioning relative to prior work.
    - Describe a specific moment a mentee's question exposed a gap in your own understanding, and how you closed it.
    - Deliver your final roadmap retrospective, unscripted.
